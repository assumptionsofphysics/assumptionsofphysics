% Abstract (Do not insert blank lines, i.e. \\) 
\abstract{Reverse Physics is a methodology that breaks physical theories into separate mathematical and physical conditions to establish their logical relationships. To showcase the power of the methodology, we present several results for quantum mechanics and their related insights. The standard Hilbert-space formulation conflicts with basic physical requirements, while a minimal topological modification can solve these problems. The ensemble space, rather than the pure-state space, distinguishes classical from quantum systems. The Born rule is an additional assumption linking orthogonality, mutual exclusivity and information entropy. Under explicit background conditions, unitary evolution is equivalent to deterministic and reversible evolution. Nonselective projective measurements can be characterized as Lindblad equilibration processes, while unitary evolution can be characterized as a limit of infinitesimal projective processes. Classical mechanics is recovered as the high-entropy limit of quantum mechanics, and every quantum state, pure or mixed, is a dynamic, spectral and thermodynamic equilibrium. These results are self-contained, use the standard vector-space representation and can thus be used as common tools and constraints for teaching, interpretations, reconstructions and future theories.}

\section{Introduction}

The core question of this special issue is, ``What kinds of entities does quantum mechanics refer to?'' The fact that, one hundred years after the birth of the theory, this question still has no conclusive answer \cite{SchlosshauerKoflerZeilinger2013,JedlickaEtAl2025} should force us to step back and ask, ``What methodology is being used to answer this question? Is it appropriate? How does it compare to other methodologies used in foundational disciplines?''

Interpretations and reconstructions of quantum mechanics seem to be the most used methods \cite{Myrvold2022,Grinbaum2007,Berghofer2024}, but they both suffer similar issues. First of all, they proceed as though we have already identified the appropriate mathematical entities that \emph{can} map one-to-one to physical entities, and it is just a matter of giving them physical meaning or an alternative definition. But known open problems show that we are not in that position \cite{CarcassiCalderonAidala2025,JaffeWitten2006,Gallavotti2006,Heathcote1990,Ruetsche2011,EarmanFraser2006,Redei1996,Toader2021}. In fact, we argue that a better understanding of the physics is required to inform what the correct mathematical structures should be. A second problem is that, most of the time, the ontology of an interpretation or the different starting points of a reconstruction are chosen, effectively, as a matter of preference \cite{SchlosshauerKoflerZeilinger2013,JedlickaEtAl2025,Grinbaum2007,Berghofer2024}. One may privilege information-theoretic aspects; another wants a realist answer to the measurement problem; yet another focuses on mathematical rigor \cite{Berghofer2024,AlloriEtAl2008,KronzLupher2024}, with the physics, in some cases, only appearing after 120 pages of theorems. We do not contest that everyone is entitled to their approach. The issue is that, in practice, even understanding exactly the results and limitations of one approach requires significant investment, and it is even more difficult to make comparisons. A third problem is that many claims remain specific to the approach and cannot serve as a common basis. For example, if one introduces entities with features that, by their definition, are not, directly or indirectly, experimentally accessible, their relevance to experimental physics will be limited and not universal. On the technical side, some moves work very well in finite-dimensional systems, but do not carry over to infinite-dimensional ones. Some work in the opposite direction \cite{Ruetsche2011}. Interpretations and reconstructions that are based on those moves cannot provide insights that are general. Our conclusion, then, is that these methodologies are unlikely to provide incontrovertible results that can reach a broad consensus.

If we look at the foundations of mathematics, while there are very different schools of thought on what should be accepted as mathematics, there is tacit agreement that there must be some logical system that provides well-defined sentences and rules of inference, and a set of axioms on the objects those sentences describe \cite{Horsten2022,Ferreiros2008}. One may accept or not accept the law of the excluded middle or the Axiom of Countable Choice, but all will agree that, if one or the other is accepted, it will have consequences. The aim of Reverse Physics \cite{aop-phys-ReversePhysics} is to perform a similar analysis for all physical theories, providing stable common results that one can use freely in their interpretations, reconstructions or as insights for future theories. With that in mind, Reverse Physics aims to be practical. Each result should be expressible and provable with standard tools (e.g. vector space formulation, phase-space formulation), accessible to anyone with an advanced undergraduate background in math and physics, and cleanly extractable from the overall framework.

For the past five years, we have been working on refining the methodology, to make it as close as possible to the practice of mathematical logic while retaining the ability to work with informal objects, as required by physics. In this paper, we will present the status of this process, through a series of results that have functioned as different test cases. Most of the results presented are new, and the others were reformulated using the refined methodology. We will provide only proof sketches, leaving the  full details in technical briefs available through our website \cite{AssumptionsOfPhysicsWebsite} and other works. The goal, in fact, is to showcase the methodology to those that are both interested in the foundations of physics and well-versed in formal and informal logic, making philosophers of physics particularly suited to its review and criticism.

In section two we will give a brief introduction to Reverse Physics. In section three we will see that Hilbert spaces fail to respect basic physical conditions. In section four we see a way to partly fix the vector space formulation. In section five we see that it is the convex structure of statistical ensembles that differentiates quantum from classical mechanics. In section six we see that the Born rule is independent of said structure, and is equivalent to assuming orthogonality and mutual exclusivity coincide. In section seven we see that unitary evolution is exactly deterministic and reversible evolution. In section eight we see that nonselective projective measurements are equilibrations, that they can be understood as the limit of an open evolution and that unitary evolution can be seen as an infinite sequence of infinitesimal measurements. In section nine we see that classical mechanics can be recovered as the high-entropy limit of quantum mechanics. In section ten we see that all states, including pure ones, are dynamic, statistical and approximately thermodynamic equilibria. The results follow from standard textbook mathematics and physics, without interpretation-specific assumptions, thus clarifying what we are committing ourselves to when we use the theory. As such, they can inform all interpretations and reconstructions.

On the practical side, one is left with two choices. 


\section{Introduction to Reverse Physics}

In the foundations of mathematics, a common fruitful strategy is to analyze the logical dependencies of different statements over a set of preconditions \cite{Simpson2009,Stillwell2018}. A famous example is the following \cite{Pinter2014,Schechter1997}:
\begin{Theorem}
	Over Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory, the following are equivalent:
	\begin{description}
		\item[AoC] (Axiom of Choice) For every non-empty set $X$ there exists a function $f$ that assigns to each nonempty set $S \subseteq X$ some
		representative element $f ( S ) \in S$;
		\item [WOP] (Well-Ordering Principle) Every set can be well-ordered;
		\item [ZL] (Zorn's Lemma) Every non-empty partially ordered set $P$ with the property that every chain in $P$ is bounded has a maximal element.
	\end{description}
\end{Theorem}
These types of investigations allow one to better understand the structure of mathematics and clarify what each starting point commits us to, particularly when intuition can be faulty. As Jerry Bona declared \cite[p. 145]{Schechter1997} regarding intuition about these three equivalent statements:
\begin{quote}
	``The Axiom of Choice is obviously true, the well-ordering principle obviously false, and who can tell about Zorn's lemma?''
\end{quote}
In particular, reverse mathematics is a program in mathematical logic that studies what axioms are necessary in order to prove theorems in ordinary mathematics.

In a similar vein, Reverse Physics aims to break physical theories into a set of mathematical and physical conditions and study their logical relationships.  Paraphrasing Friedman, the remarkable observation is that ``when the math is derived from the right physical assumptions, the physical assumptions can be derived from the math.'' The axioms and definitions of the mathematical models we use in physics can, and should, be understood as physical commitments, from the methodological to simplifying assumptions for the system under study. Whether an ontological commitment is required by a theory, then, is a question of whether it follows logically from that theory. Whether a reconstruction is valid, even in the infinite-dimensional case, is a question as to whether the starting point implies the theory over some background theory. Additionally, we can study the equivalence between multiple conditions at the same time, extending our intuition and understanding. The goal is to establish all these relationships within the same framework, with common standards and conventions. Consequently, the program is being run as an open research program, with all results and methodology guidelines, as they evolve, collected into a single open source book.

While the spirit of Reverse Physics is, as we confirmed during discussions with experts, similar to that of reverse mathematics, the techniques have to be different. This should not be surprising since physics is not math. The main difference is that math is, at least in its modern incarnation, a formal game. Physics deals with objects in the real world and experimental evidence, which are not formal objects. Therefore, many of the techniques developed exist to tame the informality, find bridges between the formal and the informal practice where it makes sense, and leave arguments informal when they should be. The line is found by experimentation, to see what works and what doesn't, and not by some a-priori aesthetic standard. The good news is that, after almost a decade of trying different strategies, patterns are emerging. The bad news is that, as with any technique, one can only truly appreciate its ins and outs by using it on a broad set of problems, not by reading about it.

Since the primitive notions in physics, like physical systems, physical processes or statistical ensembles, are always informal, they cannot be fully formalized because they play different roles in different contexts. In the same way that human beings can be characterized as having two legs in some contexts and not in others, what an electron is has changed over time, will likely change and it has different characterizations in different contexts. Since this is the de-facto practice in physics, our methodology has to follow, so that one can understand which results carry over as the concepts evolve, and which do not. We found, then, that conditions should never define what the objects are, but rather describe how some physical objects are characterized mathematically in a specific context, or how a physical notion relates to another physical notion. This way, one does not make complete commitments, but rather partial commitments that make sense in a limited context. This not only avoids most problems related to informality, but also forces one to find the minimal claims that are needed to establish the relationship between different conditions. In practice, this seems to work very well.

The presence of informal notions means that the statements of the conditions, as well as the proof, will contain some informal elements. These will be kept to a minimum, as they do represent the more critical parts, but they will have to be present. Therefore, one should not expect to have either a complete formal specification of all conditions or a fully formal proof. Again, this is physics, and not math.

Having a fully formalized argument, in fact, is neither necessary nor sufficient for the argument to hold physically, and, in most cases, is not desirable. It is not sufficient because providing a formal definition does not mean that the definition is capturing the physics: there has to be a physical justification for the formal definition, and that will be informal. It is not necessary because if, in the end, it is the physical justification of the formal definition that supports the argument, we logically can skip the middle formalization. It is not desirable because if our goal is to develop our physical understanding and intuition, this will be better accomplished by arguments that can remain fully in the informal domain. The value of formal verification, then, lies in checking the consistency and consequences of our physical intuition. Such checks do not require every physical notion and every step of the physical argument to be fully formalized, but rather should be tailored to the context at hand.

The presence of informal elements raises the question of what standards of evidence are appropriate for assessing the argument. In experimental physics, no individual result establishes a physical claim with complete certainty. Confidence develops through converging evidence obtained using different techniques and by different investigators. A mathematical conjecture remains unproved in the absence of a proof, regardless of the number of plausible arguments supporting it. In physics, however, multiple sources of evidence can progressively justify regarding a claim as confirmed. We propose applying this same evidential practice to theoretical physics. We are not going to rely on a single foolproof argument, which cannot exist, but rather on a network of different conditions and arguments that point in the same direction. In Reverse Physics, establishing a network of relationships among different conditions provides a means of carrying out this triangulation.

%The mathematical inclined, the one that wants everything to be fully specified with a tight argument all the way through will likely be horrified and see no point in all this: a single weak point is enough to undermine the whole argument. So, what is the point? If one takes this attitude to its natural conclusions, all of science is invalid anyway. In fact, physics is about experiments, and no experiment gives us anything with certainty. An unreasonable doubt (i.e. it's all a coincidence) always exists and cannot be removed. The issue is that physics, or engineering, or any other discipline that has to do with the real world, already bakes the premise that no single argument or piece of evidence is conclusive. Truth is found by triangulation from different pieces of evidence, taken with different techniques and by different people. While in math, a conjecture supported by ten different arguments is still an unproven theorem, in physics it gradually become something we regard as confirmed. In a way, we are simply applying the same standards in theoretical physics that are used in experimental physics, making the discipline more consistent across experimental and theoretical practice. The conclusion, then, is that we should not be at all satisfied by a single argument, because we can never be certain about an informal argument, but rather we should seek different connections, and triangulate. Again, it will be the web of relationship between different conditions that will increase our confidence in the results.

As an example, here we present a result that characterizes time evolution in quantum mechanics as determinism and reversibility. We will have three different characterizations: one in terms of bijective maps between pure states, one in terms of conservation of the Born rule and one in terms of conservation of entropy. While these are different characterizations, one can see how, intuitively, they should be connected. In fact, similar results in classical mechanics gave us the intuition that these results should hold for quantum mechanics as well. If a system undergoes deterministic and reversible evolution, we should definitely have a one-to-one correspondence between initial and final states. Moreover, determinism and reversibility should imply that we should have, at least in principle, corresponding measurements on the initial and final states. Lastly, if the evolution is deterministic and reversible, we should expect that the amount of information given about the initial state should match the amount of information given about the final state. What we are going to do is show that this intuitive relationship exists in the math as well. We are effectively triangulating our intuition against the math.

Note that, once these equivalences are established, we can add different modes of experimental confirmation. Without the connection, we typically confirm the equation directly: we have a system, we assume some Hamiltonian, and find that it evolves according to the given equation. If we establish that unitary evolution is determinism and reversibility, then an experimenter can check whether that assumption is valid or not in his setup. This could be done, for example, by characterizing how much the system is isolated. This extra confirmation is another form of triangulation that tests that the equivalence found in the theoretical setting also exists in the experimental one.

\section{Mathematical prerequisites}

In this section we prove a key mathematical proposition that will be used later in the theorem. In the most general setting, a process can be characterized by a map from an initial ensemble to a final ensemble. This map will be continuous if and only if small changes of the input correspond to small changes of the output. Moreover, it will be affine if and only if the process preserves statistical mixing. Lastly, bijectivity of the map will be related to determinism and reversibility. Since, in quantum mechanics, ensembles are represented by density operators, it will be useful to characterize bijective continuous affine maps between density operators in multiple ways.

The key insight is that affinity forces the bijectivity on pure states to be equivalent to the bijectivity on density operators. Since density operators are trace-class and self-adjoint, they can always be expressed as a convex countable mixture of pure states. Since the map is affine and continuous, the mixture of the output is the output of the mixture. Therefore, the map on the pure states defines the map on all mixtures and vice-versa: a continuous affine map is bijective on pure states if and only if it is bijective on all mixed states.

Moreover, bijective continuous affine maps can always be represented as conjugation by unitary or anti-unitary action on the Hilbert space. This is because the preservation of affine combinations forces the Born rule to be conserved. Given that pure states are uniquely represented by rays, and not vectors, expressing the requirements in terms of projectors and density operators allows one to more clearly map physics requirements to the vector space representation.

\begin{Proposition}[Affine pure-state correspondence]\label{AFF-PURE}
	Let $\mathcal{H}$ be a nonzero complex Hilbert space, and let
	$D(\mathcal{H})$ be its space of density operators. Denote by
	$\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{H})$ its pure states, represented by rank-one
	orthogonal projectors. Let
	$T:D(\mathcal{H})\to D(\mathcal{H})$
	be a trace-norm continuous affine map. Then the following are equivalent:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $T$ is bijective on all density operators;
		\item $T$ is bijective on all pure states;
		\item $T$ maps pure states onto pure states and preserves their
		Born transition probabilities (i.e. $	\tr\bigl(T(P)T(Q)\bigr)=\tr(PQ)$ ) for every $P,Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{H})$;
		\item there exists either a unitary or an anti-unitary operator $W:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}$ such that $T(\rho)=W\rho W^\dagger$ for every $\rho\in D(\mathcal{H})$.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{Proposition}

\begin{proof}
	We prove the proposition with the following order of implications $1\implies2\implies3\implies4\implies1$.
	Note that if $\dim\mathcal{H}=1$, the density-operator space is a singleton
	and all four conditions hold. We therefore assume
	$\dim\mathcal{H}\geq2$.
	
	Let us first show that $1\implies2$. The idea is that an affine
	bijection preserves whether a state admits a nontrivial
	decomposition into a statistical mixture. Since both the map
	and its inverse preserve mixing, such a decomposition can
	be transported in either direction. Since pure states are exactly those states that do not admit nontrivial decompositions, states are mapped bijectively.
	
	More explicitly, suppose $T$ is bijective on all density
	operators. Its inverse is affine because $T$ is affine.
	Let $P$ be pure and suppose
	$T(P)=\lambda\rho+(1-\lambda)\sigma$, with $0<\lambda<1$.
	Applying the inverse gives
	\begin{equation}
		P=\lambda T^{-1}(\rho)+(1-\lambda)T^{-1}(\sigma).
	\end{equation}
	Since $P$ is pure,
	$T^{-1}(\rho)=T^{-1}(\sigma)=P$.
	Consequently, $\rho=\sigma=T(P)$, so $T(P)$ is pure.
	Applying the same argument to $T^{-1}$ shows that the
	preimage of every pure state is pure. Thus $T$ maps pure
	states bijectively onto pure states.

	We now show that $2\implies3$. The idea is to examine the mixtures supported on a two-dimensional subspace. These form a Bloch ball, whose surface consists of pure states. We will show that affinity and injectivity on pure states force the induced map to preserve the geometry of this ball. Since every pair of distinct pure states is supported on a two-dimensional subspace, this will establish preservation of all Born transition probabilities.

	Let $E$ be two-dimensional, with orthogonal pure states $P,Q$ spanning it, and set
	\begin{equation}
	\tau=\frac{I_E}{2}=\frac{P+Q}{2}.
	\end{equation}
	Since $T(P)$ and $T(Q)$ are distinct pure states, their ranges span a two-dimensional subspace $F$, which is the support of $T(\tau)$.

	Every density operator $\rho$ supported on $E$ satisfies
	\begin{equation}
	\tau=\frac{\rho+(I_E-\rho)}{2},
	\end{equation}
	where $I_E-\rho$ is also a density operator. Affinity and positivity therefore imply that $T(\rho)$ is supported on $F$: neither positive summand can have support outside the support of their sum. Thus $T$ maps the Bloch ball on $E$ into the Bloch ball on $F$.

	Choose Bloch coordinates on both spaces. Affinity gives
	\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{r}\longmapsto\mathbf{a}+M\mathbf{r},
	\end{equation}
	with $M$ a real $3\times3$ matrix. Pure states correspond to unit vectors. Injectivity on pure states makes $M$ invertible: otherwise, opposite unit vectors in its kernel would have the same image.

	Since both $\mathbf{r}$ and $-\mathbf{r}$ represent pure states,
	\begin{equation}
	\|\mathbf{a}+M\mathbf{r}\|^2
	=\|\mathbf{a}-M\mathbf{r}\|^2=1
	\qquad\text{for }\|\mathbf{r}\|=1.
	\end{equation}
	Subtracting gives $\mathbf{a}\cdot M\mathbf{r}=0$ for every unit vector $\mathbf{r}$. Invertibility of $M$ then forces $\mathbf{a}=0$. Consequently, $M$ preserves the unit sphere and, by linearity, all lengths and inner products.

	For pure states $R,S$ with Bloch vectors $\mathbf{r},\mathbf{s}$,
	\begin{equation}
	\tr(RS)=\frac{1+\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{s}}{2}.
	\end{equation}
	Preservation of the inner product therefore gives $\tr(T(R)T(S))=\tr(RS)$. Every pair of distinct pure states is supported on a two-dimensional subspace, so this holds throughout $\mathcal{H}$. Surjectivity on pure states is already assumed, completing $2\implies3$.
	
	Next, let us show that $3\implies4$. This is, effectively, an application of Wigner's theorem, which states that bijective ray transformations preserving Born transition probabilities can be implemented by unitary or anti-unitary operators.
	
	The Born transition probabilities determine whether two pure states coincide: $\tr(PQ)=1$ if and only if $P=Q$. Their preservation therefore makes $T$ injective on pure states. Together with the assumed surjectivity, this gives a bijection of the pure-state space preserving all transition probabilities.
	
	Wigner's theorem now applies
	\cite[Theorem~5.29]{Landsman2017}: there is a unitary or anti-unitary operator $W:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}$ such that
	\begin{equation}
		T(P)=WPW^\dagger
	\end{equation}
	for every pure state $P$. It remains to extend this representation to all density operators.
	
	Every density operator has a finite or countable spectral
	decomposition
	\begin{equation}
		\rho=\sum_n p_nP_n,
		\qquad
		p_n>0,
		\qquad
		\sum_n p_n=1,
	\end{equation}
	where the $P_n$ are mutually orthogonal pure states. For a finite sum, affinity immediately gives
	\begin{equation}
		T(\rho)
		=\sum_n p_nT(P_n)
		=W\rho W^\dagger.
	\end{equation}
	For an infinite sum, set
	\begin{equation}
		s_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}p_n,
		\qquad
		\rho_N=\frac{1}{s_N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}p_nP_n.
	\end{equation}
	These are density operators, and
	$\|\rho_N-\rho\|_1=2(1-s_N)\to0$.
	The finite-mixture argument gives
	$T(\rho_N)=W\rho_NW^\dagger$.
	By continuity of $T$ and trace-norm continuity of unitary
	or anti-unitary conjugation, taking the limit gives
	\begin{equation}
		T(\rho)=W\rho W^\dagger.
	\end{equation}
	This proves $3\implies4$.
	
	Finally, $4\implies1$. This step is just a verification that unitary and anti-unitary conjugation give us a bijective map on all density operators.
	
	Unitary and anti-unitary conjugation preserve positivity and trace, so both $\rho\mapsto W\rho W^\dagger$ and $\rho\mapsto W^\dagger\rho W$ map density operators to density operators. These maps are inverses of one another. Hence $T$ is bijective on all density operators, completing the cycle of implications.
\end{proof}

These results should not be surprising to those familiar with the literature, as they are present, in one form or another, in many places. They work in finite as well as infinite dimensions. In finite dimensions, it suffices that $T$ is injective on the pure states $P(\mathcal{H})$, as the compactness of $P(\mathcal{H})$ will force $T$ to be bijective on $P(\mathcal{H})$. In infinite dimensions, this does not work: the map $|n\> \mapsto |n+1\>$, which shifts the energy levels of a harmonic oscillator, is a counterexample.

\section{Conditions}

The next step is to establish the conditions needed for the theorem. In this case, we will have a typical equivalence theorem: a set of background conditions is assumed and, on top of those, we prove the equivalence of different conditions. In this section we go through these conditions, showing why the wording on some is critical.

The first background condition represents the standard structure of quantum states on Hilbert space. This effectively brings in the standard vector space representation used in quantum mechanics.
\begin{condition}[Quantum states]{QST:H}
	The state space of a quantum system is represented by the projective space $\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{H})$ of a separable complex Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$. The ensemble space is represented by the density operators $\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})$. The conditional probability is given by the Born rule $p(\psi|\phi) = \frac{\< \phi | \psi \>\< \psi | \phi \>}{\< \psi | \psi \>\< \phi | \phi \>}$. The entropy is given by the von Neumann entropy $S(\rho) = -\tr(\rho \log \rho)$.
\end{condition}
The topologies are the usual ones. In particular, the weak operator topology and the trace-norm topology coincide on the space of density operators \cite[Lemma~11.1]{Holevo2019}.

Note that this does not provide a definition for quantum states. We recognize that states, systems, measurements, and so on, are all informal notions that depend on context, which will change in time. This is true for most concepts in science and in everyday speech. We are only saying that quantum states can be represented by elements of a projective space $\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{H})$: each state corresponds to a different element. This is the only feature that is used about quantum systems; therefore everything else is left undefined. We are not saying that the representation is faithful, that every element corresponds to a physical state. We are not assuming an ontology for quantum states, or for complex numbers. We are not assuming a specific version of set theory, or category theory, as a foundation. Any that will allow one to specify the condition will do. In the future, we do want to understand what minimal mathematical axioms are required to express these concepts, to have a solid connection to reverse mathematics. But, for many reasons, it is premature at this point.

The condition tacitly implies that the representation is a choice, which is, again, just a recognition of how things are. One could use Wigner functions, Husimi Q functions, or any other pseudo-probability representation. One could also use the probability density and phase. One could imagine, in fact, having a condition QST:W for the first, QST:Q for the second, and so on, and then have a theorem that shows the equivalence of those representations. This would fit the spirit of the overall reverse physics program, and could serve as a concise summary for anyone who starts to work on quantum foundations. Note that the different proposed names imply that the same notion (i.e. \emph{Q}uantum\emph{ST}ates) is being represented in different ways (i.e. \emph{H}ilbert spaces, \emph{W}igner functions). While these rules are not yet finalized, we find it useful to keep track of all different conditions and their relationship.

The next base condition tells us that time evolution preserves statistical mixtures: the evolution of a mixture is the mixture of the evolutions of the individual components.
\begin{condition}[Evolution preserves statistical mixtures]{[B]EVMIX}
	The evolution of a statistical mixture is the statistical mixture of the evolutions. That is, let $\mathcal{D}$ be the ensemble space of the system; the evolution from each $t_0$ to each $t_1$, with $t_1\geq t_0$, is given by an affine map $\Phi_{t_0 \to t_1}:\mathcal{D}\to \mathcal{D}$ such that $\Phi_{t_0 \to t_0}=I$, $\Phi_{t_1 \to t_2}\circ \Phi_{t_0 \to t_1}=\Phi_{t_0 \to t_2}$ and $(\rho,t_0,t_1)\mapsto \Phi_{t_0 \to t_1}(\rho)$ is jointly continuous.
\end{condition}
Note that the condition is prefixed with [B], which indicates a base condition for physics. In reverse mathematics, the base theory (typically RCA0) is effectively the background theory for the whole subject. It's the minimum you need to do anything. Part of the problem in the foundations of physics is that we have no such base theory for physics. We do not have a standard characterization for states, processes or physical quantities that all other physical theories simply specialize. One of the goals of reverse physics, in fact, is to find such a base theory for physics. As we disentangle the ball of yarn into different conditions, we can ask whether one is so fundamental that all physical theories must follow. In this case, if we define time evolution on the statistical ensembles of any system, since the statistical ensemble represents different chosen preparations at different times, the evolution of each instance cannot depend on the evolution of the other. Therefore, $\Phi(p\rho + (1-p)\sigma) = p \Phi(\rho) + (1-p) \Phi(\sigma)$. Time evolution, then, must be affine. This is why the condition is prefixed with [B] and it is expressed in a way to not depend on quantum mechanics specifically.

Note the condition holds because statistical ensembles were understood as statistical mixtures, but an ensemble can represent different things. If it represents, for example, the different concentrations of two elements present in a flask undergoing an autocatalytic reaction, the evolution will not be affine. Current physics language often does not make these distinctions clear or easy to make clear. In our view, improving the language surrounding these notions is even more important than clarifying mathematical dependencies, as they impact which physical models are valid and which aren't. Again, the goal of reverse physics is to tease out these implicit assumptions, make them explicit and, most importantly, organize them.

As the last background condition, we are going to restrict the dynamics to the time-independent case.
\begin{condition}[Time-independent evolution]{EVTI}
	Evolution depends only on elapsed time. That is, $\Phi_{t_0 \to t_1}=\Phi_{t_1-t_0}$.
\end{condition}
This is not a fundamental limitation of the equivalence we are going to show, but rather a simplifying condition to make our proofs easier to digest.

Having specified and discussed the background conditions, let us present and analyze the five conditions we want to prove equivalent. First, the Schrödinger equation.
\begin{condition}[Schrödinger equation]{DR-SCEQ}
	The evolution follows the equation $\frac{d}{dt} \psi(t) = \frac{H(t)}{\imath \hbar} \psi(t)$, where $H(t)$ is a self-adjoint operator.
\end{condition}
Note that the condition still allows for time-dependent evolution. As the goal is to develop a common dependency tree for all conditions, we do not want to have two separate conditions: one for a family $H(t)$ and one for a single $H$. It will be \ref{EVTI} that will allow us to choose a single time-independent Hamiltonian, in the same way that it will constrain all other conditions.

Also note that we are not making domains explicit. First, because these are often not made explicit, and the average physicist may think we are not using the standard expression, but one with caveats. Second, because the domain problem exists in a Hilbert space but does not exist, for example, in a Schwartz space. As we said before, there is no assumption that the Hilbert space representation is faithful. It makes sense, then, to tacitly assume that the conditions are implicitly expressed on those states that are faithful, rather than remarking it every time in each condition. The unspecified parts are available for a convenient mathematical completion because the physics does not constrain them. These are exactly the kind of mathematical details that should be analyzed independently, to see whether the mathematical representation could be improved or clarified, but ultimately should not appear in the conditions because they are not part of the physics.

The second condition to study is unitary evolution.
\begin{condition}[Unitary evolution]{DR-UNIT}
	Time evolution is represented by a strongly continuous one-parameter group of unitary operators. That is, $\Phi_t(\rho)=U_t\rho U_t^\dagger$, where $U_t:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}$, $U_0=I$, $U_{t+s}=U_tU_s$ and $U_t^\dagger U_t=U_tU_t^\dagger=I$.
\end{condition}
This characterization of time evolution is the one preferred on the mathematical side. The equivalence to the previous condition is well known, and it connects more directly to the next characterizations of time evolution. This is also the element that would become more involved in the time-dependent case. Effectively, one needs the time evolution to be differentiable so that generators exist at all times. The corresponding physical assumption is that the evolution map varies ``slowly,'' so that, around each time $t$, there is a time-independent evolution that agrees to first order with the time-dependent one. Therefore the proof would still first be restricted to the time-independent case, and then generalized to this case. The interesting part there, then, would be the equivalence between different characterizations of differentiability for time evolution.

We finally introduce the first physical condition: deterministic and reversible evolution in terms of bijective maps.
\begin{condition}[Bijective map]{DR-EV}
	Distinct past states map to distinct future states and vice-versa. That is, if $\psi_I$ and $\phi_I$ are two initial states, $\psi_F$ and $\phi_F$ are the corresponding evolved future states and $\psi_P$ and $\phi_P$ are the corresponding reconstructed past states, then $\psi_I = \phi_I \iff \psi_F = \phi_F \iff \psi_P = \phi_P$.
\end{condition}
The reader will have noticed the DR prefix for all these conditions: it stands for determinism and reversibility. The idea is that the assumption of determinism and reversibility can be expressed in several equivalent conditions. Therefore, all the conditions with the DR prefix are not only equivalent, but represent the equivalence class corresponding to the determinism and reversibility assumption.

Note that the wording of the condition is carefully worded so that it solves a crucial problem while not attracting attention to it. One may think that requiring that two distinct initial states are mapped to two distinct final states is enough to require determinism and reversibility. It is not. This just imposes a bijection only on the image of the map (i.e. the final states reached by the map). Consider the map $|n\> \mapsto |n+1\>$, which shifts the energy levels of a harmonic oscillator. This does map distinct initial states to distinct final states, but the ground state ``disappears.'' This also means that the ground has no past: if we started in the ground state, where did it come from?

To make the condition truly symmetric with respect to time, the idea is that any present state must have both a future and a past, and both must be uniquely determined by the present state. Because of the time independence, we can take the present to be the future, for which the past is the present. This now establishes a bijection between initial and final states. Talking about past, present and future states, then, is not redundant, but actually makes the relationship more clear in all the physical characterizations.

The second physical condition is in terms of the Born rule.
\begin{condition}[Preservation of conditional probability]{DR-PROB}
	Conditional probability remains the same across past and future states. That is, if $\psi_I$ and $\phi_I$ are two initial states, $\psi_F$ and $\phi_F$ are the corresponding evolved future states and $\psi_P$ and $\phi_P$ are the corresponding reconstructed past states, then $p(\psi_I \mid \phi_I) = p(\psi_F \mid \phi_F) = p(\psi_P \mid \phi_P)$.
\end{condition}
As before, we have pairs of states that are evolved and reconstructed in time. The difference is that it is not equality that is conserved, but their conditional probability. That is, in the context of quantum mechanics, we can characterize how the Born rule changes in time, and we can request that it stays the same.

The last condition is in terms of the entropy.
\begin{condition}[Conservation of information entropy]{DR-INFO}
	Future and past ensembles have the same information entropy. That is, if $\rho_I$ is an initial ensemble, $\rho_F$ is the corresponding evolved future ensemble and $\rho_P$ is the corresponding reconstructed past ensemble, then $S(\rho_I) = S(\rho_F) = S(\rho_P)$.
\end{condition}
Conceptually, we are requiring that the information needed to identify the elements of the mixture remains unchanged in time: the information I give you on the input is exactly the same information that I gave you on the output. The connection to determinism and reversibility should, therefore, not be surprising. What is interesting, however, is understanding exactly what the condition is saying.

This time, we are only evolving one ensemble at a time, not comparing two. However, we need to understand what is being mapped, and it is easier with an example. Suppose we have a classical particle under Brownian motion, such that, if we started exactly at the origin, after some time the particle would spread according to a Gaussian. The more time passes, the more the Gaussian spreads. Now, the reconstructed past ensemble is not the inverse of the map. In fact, in general, a final distribution could have come from different initial distributions. We are asking a different question: suppose I know that the particle is exactly at the origin; what can I tell about its past position? In the same way that we can't tell exactly where the particle is going to be, we can't tell exactly where it came from. The reconstructed past state is also a Gaussian, with the spread increased the further we go into the past. Mathematically, we have the event $X_0 = 0$ which corresponds to the set $A$ of all the trajectories that pass through position zero at time zero. Then we take the probability distribution of the variable $X_t$ conditioned on $A$. But since the dynamics is symmetric in time, the probability distribution for $X_t$ and $X_{-t}$ will be the same, with the variance given by $2D|t|$ where $D$ is the diffusion coefficient.

What the condition is imposing, then, is that these future and past distributions have the same entropy as the initial distribution. And, hopefully, it should be clear why it works, at least in this Brownian motion example. Since spreading of the distribution increases entropy, this cannot happen. Therefore the condition imposes that the diffusion coefficient is $0$.


\section{Theorem}

Having gone through all the conditions, we can now state the theorem.

\begin{Theorem}
	Over \ref{QST:H}+\ref{[B]EVMIX}+\ref{EVTI}, conditions \ref{DR-SCEQ}, \ref{DR-UNIT}, \ref{DR-EV}, \ref{DR-PROB} and \ref{DR-INFO} are equivalent.
\end{Theorem}

\begin{proof}
	The background is set by the conjunction of the first three conditions. Therefore, throughout the theorem, we are assuming the Hilbert space characterization of quantum states and that the time evolution map is affine over ensembles and constant in time. To show the equivalence, we have to assume each condition independently and show that the others can derived from it.
	
	We first show that all the physical conditions imply unitary evolution. The we show that unitary evolution implies all three physical condition. Lastly, we show that unitary evolution is equivalent to the Schrödinger equation.
	
	\ref{DR-EV} $\implies$ \ref{DR-UNIT}. First, we show that \ref{DR-EV} gives us a continuous bijective map on pure states between two fixed times. By \ref{EVTI}, there exists a family of maps $\Phi_t : \mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}) \to \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D})$ that evolves a pure state for an elapsed time $t$. By \ref{DR-EV}, the evolution of a pure state is a pure state, and pure states can be reconstructed using the evolution. Consider $\psi_1 = \Phi_t(\psi_0)$. Then $\psi_1$ is the future state corresponding to the initial state $\psi_0$. Moreover, $\psi_0$ is a past state corresponding to the initial state $\psi_1$. Similarly for $\phi_1 = \Phi_t(\phi_0)$. By \ref{DR-EV}, $\psi_0 = \phi_0$ if and only if $\psi_1 = \phi_1$, which also means that the reconstructed state must be unique. Since every state admits an evolution and a reconstruction, $\Phi_t$ maps pure states bijectively onto pure states. By \ref{[B]EVMIX}, $\Phi_t$ is continuous and affine. Since $\Phi_t$ is continuous, affine and is a bijection on pure states, by proposition \ref{AFF-PURE} it is implemented by either a unitary or an anti-unitary operator.
	
	Next, we show that the family of all fixed time evolution can be represented by a unitary group. The anti-unitary case is excluded by composition. In fact, $\Phi_t=\Phi_{t/2}\circ \Phi_{t/2}$, and the composition of two maps implemented by an anti-unitary is implemented by a unitary. Therefore every $\Phi_t$ is implemented by a unitary. Since the evolution maps are bijective trace-norm isometries, defining $\Phi_{-t}=\Phi_t^{-1}$ extends them to a continuous group. The composition law then gives a continuous projective unitary representation of $(\mathbb{R},+)$. By the lifting theorem for this group \cite[Theorem~5.59]{Landsman2017}, the phases can be chosen so that the implementing operators form a strongly continuous one-parameter unitary group, with $U_0=I$, $U_{t+s}=U_tU_s$ and $U_{-t}=U_t^\dagger$. Therefore \ref{DR-UNIT} holds.
	
	\ref{DR-PROB} $\implies$ \ref{DR-UNIT}. We show that preserving the Born rule is equivalent to preserving equality of state. In fact, two states are the same if and only if their conditional probability is one. Therefore
	\begin{equation}
		\psi_I=\phi_I
		\iff p(\psi_I\mid\phi_I)=1
		\iff p(\psi_F\mid\phi_F)=1
		\iff \psi_F=\phi_F,
	\end{equation}
	and the same argument applies to the reconstructed past states. Therefore \ref{DR-PROB} implies \ref{DR-EV}, which implies \ref{DR-UNIT} by the previous argument.
	
	\ref{DR-INFO} $\implies$ \ref{DR-UNIT}. We show that conservation of entropy implies preserving equality on pure states. First, entropy conservation means pure states must be mapped to pure states. Second, affinity and entropy conservation leads to the preservation of the Jensen--Shannon divergence, which forces the conservation of the identity of pure states.
	
	Recall that pure ensembles are exactly the ensembles with zero von Neumann entropy. Let $\psi_I \in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{H})$ be a pure state and $\psi_F, \psi_P \in \mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})$ be the, possible mixed, evolution and reconstructions. By \ref{DR-INFO}, $S(\phi_I)=S(\phi_F)=S(\phi_P)=0$, which means $\psi_F$ and $\psi_P$ are pure states. Therefore, the evolution and reconstructions of pure states are pure state.
	
	Next, consider the quantum Jensen--Shannon divergence $QJSD(\rho, \sigma) = S\left(\frac{1}{2}\rho + \frac{1}{2}\sigma\right) - \frac{1}{2}S(\rho) - \frac{1}{2}S(\sigma)$, define on $\rho, \sigma \in \mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})$ such as all terms are finite. Since $\Phi_t$ is affine, we have
	\begin{equation}
		\begin{aligned}
			QJSD(\Phi_t(\rho), \Phi_t(\sigma)) &= S\left(\frac{1}{2}\Phi_t(\rho) + \frac{1}{2}\Phi_t(\sigma)\right) - \frac{1}{2}S(\Phi_t(\rho)) - \frac{1}{2}S(\Phi_t(\sigma)) \\
			&= S\left(\Phi_t\left(\frac{1}{2}\rho + \frac{1}{2}\sigma\right)\right) - \frac{1}{2}S(\Phi_t(\rho)) - \frac{1}{2}S(\Phi_t(\sigma)) \\
			&= S\left(\frac{1}{2}\rho + \frac{1}{2}\sigma\right) - \frac{1}{2}S(\rho) - \frac{1}{2}S(\sigma) = QJSD(\rho, \sigma).
		\end{aligned}
	\end{equation}
	If $\psi_I,\phi_I \in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{H})$ are two pure states, $\psi_I=\phi_I$ if and only if $QJSD(\psi_I, \phi_I) = S\left(\frac{1}{2}\psi_I + \frac{1}{2}\phi_I\right) = 0$: the mixture of two pure states is a pure state if and only if the two pure states are not distinct. Since $\psi_F = \Phi_t(\psi_I)$ and $\phi_F = \Phi_t(\phi_I)$.
	\begin{equation}
		\psi_I=\phi_I
		\iff QJSD(\psi_I,\phi_I)=0
		\iff QJSD(\psi_F,\phi_F)=0
		\iff \psi_F=\phi_F.
	\end{equation}
	
	For the reconstruction, for a general mixture the evolution of the reconstructed state is not the initial state because the uncertainty in the prediction and reconstruction will compound. However, since both the evolution and reconstructions are pure states, the two will coincide. For consistency between the evolution and the reconstruction map, the initial state must be a statistical component of the evolution of the reconstruction. Formally, if $\Theta_t : \mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}) \to \mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})$ is the reconstruction map over an interval $t$, we will have $\Phi_t(\Theta_t(\rho_I)) = p \rho_I + (1-p) \sigma$ with $p>0$. If the evolution maps pure states to pure states, if $\rho_I$ is a pure state, then either $\sigma = \rho_I$ or $p=1$. That means that, on pure states, the reconstruction is the inverse of evolution. Therefore, the reconstruction preserves the equality of states. Therefore, \ref{DR-EV} holds.
	
	Note how the above reasoning uses physical arguments as well as formal. That is, the relationship between the evolution and reconstruction map is not fully formalized, and physical reasoning to fill the gap. Having a fully formalized relationship, in fact, is neither necessary nor sufficient for the argument to hold, and, in this case, is not desirable. It is not sufficient because providing a formal definition does not mean that the definition is capturing the physics: there has to be a physical justification for it. It is not necessary because if, in the end, it is the physical justification of the formal definition that supports the argument, we logically can skip the middle. It is not desirable because, if we wanted to formalize the maps and their relationship, it would require a lot more space and a heavier exposition. On a practical level, then, it is better to leave the formalization for future work.
	
	\ref{DR-UNIT} $\implies$ \ref{DR-EV}+\ref{DR-PROB}+\ref{DR-INFO}. Let $\psi_I, \phi_I \in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{H})$ be two pure state. By \ref{DR-UNIT}, their corresponding evolved states are $\psi_F=U_t \psi_I U_t^{\dagger}$ and $\phi_F=U_t\phi_I U_t^{\dagger}$; their corresponding reconstructed past states $\psi_P = U_t^\dagger\psi_I U_t$ and $\phi_P = U_t^\dagger\phi_I U_t$. Since $U_t$ and $U_t^\dagger$ are bijective, \ref{DR-UNIT} implies \ref{DR-EV}. Moreover,
	\begin{equation}
		p(\psi_I\mid\phi_I)=p(\psi_F\mid\phi_F)=p(\psi_P\mid\phi_P),
	\end{equation}
	so \ref{DR-PROB} holds. Lastly, let $\rho_F=U_t\rho_IU_t^\dagger$ and $\rho_P=U_t^\dagger\rho_I U_t$. Unitary conjugation preserves eigenvalues, including multiplicities. Since the von Neumann entropy depends only on those eigenvalues,
	\begin{equation}
		S(\rho_I)=S(\rho_F)=S(\rho_P),
	\end{equation}
	and \ref{DR-INFO} holds.
	
	Lastly, \ref{DR-SCEQ} $\iff$ \ref{DR-UNIT}. The connection between unitary group and the Schrödinger equation is a well-known result. By Stone's theorem, every strongly continuous one-parameter unitary group has a unique self-adjoint generator $H$ such that
	\begin{equation}
		U_t=e^{\frac{Ht}{\imath \hbar}}.
	\end{equation}
	For every $|\psi(0)\> \in \Dom(H)$, the curve $| \psi(t) \> =U_t |\psi(0)\>$ is differentiable and satisfies \ref{DR-SCEQ}. Conversely, a time-independent self-adjoint Hamiltonian defines through the same expression a strongly continuous one-parameter unitary group. Therefore \ref{DR-SCEQ} and \ref{DR-UNIT} are equivalent, completing the proof.
\end{proof}

\section{Conclusions}

In this paper we have shown how Reverse Physics leads to a series of results on the foundations of quantum mechanics that help better characterize each element of the theory. It does so by breaking up the theory into a series of conditions, some mathematical and some physical, and studying their logical relationships. It can be used to distinguish base conditions, those that must be present in every well-defined physical theory, from those that represent additional, more restrictive assumptions. It allows one to study logical equivalence between mathematical and physical conditions, highlighting when the mathematical framework fails to be physically significant. Most importantly, all the results stand on their own as self-contained theorems, and are formulated within the standard vector-space representation of quantum mechanics, making them accessible to the widest possible audience. Moreover, similar results are available for classical mechanics, allowing cross-theory comparisons.

This means that the results can be used in many different ways for many different goals. The teachers or students who simply want to have a better understanding of the framework, the philosophers who want to develop their interpretation and extend the theory with a well-defined ontology, the researchers who want to reconstruct quantum theory from different premises, the physicists who want to develop a new theory: all of them can benefit from a more detailed understanding of the relationships among the many different conditions studied. As the methodology matures, we hope that many other results can be incorporated with the same style, making it much easier to share fundamental insights and results.
